Forecast Benchmark

Measured against the forecast you already have.

Every US balancing authority publishes its own day-ahead demand forecast through EIA-930, free. This page scores GridPulse against that forecast — on the same hours, against the same settled actual, under rules written down before the results were read.

The exclusions, the drop counts and the known limits are published here too. They are the part that makes the rest worth reading.

Excluded, and why

A balancing authority is left out only when it cannot be scored fairly — a feed whose readings revise too heavily to score intraday, or one that publishes a day-ahead forecast too rarely. Note the direction: most of these are broken feeds, and a BA with sloppy data operations plausibly also forecasts sloppily, so this list likely removes cases favourable to us rather than unfavourable ones.

What this does not show

Written down because a benchmark without them is marketing. The full list lives in the methodology.

The 24-hour arm is not lead-matched

Our realized lead measures 23.80–23.95h across a 15-BA sample, inside the 17–41h window EIA documents for day-ahead submissions. On a typical hour the operator plausibly forecast from further out than we did — on the arm every comparison here is taken from.

The hour set depends on our availability too

An hour is dropped when we have no matured prediction for it, even though the operator's forecast for that hour exists. Not corrected for; the drop counts are published per row instead.

This is not how operators forecast

The day-ahead value is what a BA publishes to EIA. It need not be the forecast it dispatches on, and several operate more sophisticated internal models. A large error here is evidence about the published series, nothing more.

Not a claim about market value

Demand error only. Nothing here is scored on prices, reserve margins, or dispatch outcomes, and two leads is not the whole horizon curve.

Rules that shaped the numbers

  • Hours where EIA published the forecast as the actual are excluded — scoring them would credit the operator with a perfect prediction on hours it never made.
  • The operator's column is the earliest day-ahead forecast we observed, not necessarily the first they issued: a revision landing before we first saw the hour is invisible to us.
  • Only settled actuals count. First-published values run up to ~70% wrong on high-revision feeds.
  • Hours we first saw after they had already passed are excluded: their earliest-observed forecast is already a post-revision value, so scoring it as “as-issued” would erase the distinction the two operator columns exist to draw.
  • The operator's forecast is scored twice — as-issued and as-revised — because a value revised after the target hour carries hindsight.
  • Verdicts are decided on mean MAPE alone. The other statistics are published for interpretation and decide nothing.
  • A row marked rollback is one our own drift monitor already grades as failing at that horizon — the number stands, but we would not defend the forecast behind it.
  • A row marked not served scores our ensemble for a BA we serve a seasonal-naive baseline instead, so its published number is worse than what that BA actually receives. The arm is not re-based onto the fallback, because that would stop it measuring the forecaster.
